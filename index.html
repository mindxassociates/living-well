2

Recognize the pattern

Understanding common changes can reduce fear and help loved ones know when to focus on comfort and presence.

A useful way to think about the final phase: energy gradually narrows. A person may become more tired, sleep more, spend less time awake, and eventually become deeply unconscious.

Individual illnesses and people vary, so this is not a timetable or a prediction. It is a framework for understanding that the body can gradually slow down.

As energy declines

  • Activity may become shorter and more tiring.
  • Rest and sleep may become increasingly important.
  • Time awake may gradually decrease.
  • Near the end, a person may be unconscious much of the time.

Near the end

  • Breathing may change in depth, speed, and rhythm.
  • There may be pauses between breaths.
  • Breathing can gradually become quieter and stop.
  • The process described in the source is often much gentler than people fear.
Important: Changes in breathing, alertness, restlessness, pain, or other symptoms should be interpreted by the person's healthcare team. Do not use this guide to diagnose or manage symptoms on your own.
What about a sudden burst of energy?

Sometimes a person who has been declining may have an unexpected temporary increase in energy or activity. The source describes this as sometimes occurring before a final decline, while also showing that medication side effects can mimic “energy” through severe restlessness. The key lesson is to look carefully at the whole picture rather than assuming a sudden change means recovery.

3

Everyone copes differently

Coping styles are not moral grades. A strategy that protects one person can frustrate another.

Control

“Tell me the plan.”

Some people cope by gathering information, organizing details, and trying to stay in control.

Avoidance

“I don't want to think about it.”

Some people cope by turning attention elsewhere or limiting contact with frightening information.

Helplessness

“I can't do this alone.”

Feeling unable to act can be painful, but it can also create space for others to provide care and support.

Acceptance

“I will meet what comes.”

Some people focus on accepting what cannot be changed and adapting to each stage.

Resilience

“What can I still do?”

Some people adapt creatively, preserve routines, and find meaningful activities within new limits.

Anxious focus

“What if something goes wrong?”

Some people become highly alert to threats and repeatedly seek reassurance or information.

When coping styles collide

A planner may feel abandoned by someone who avoids the subject. An avoidant person may feel pressured by a planner. Neither is automatically “coping better.” The goal is to notice the difference, name it, and find a workable middle ground.

Try this: Which style sounds most like you when life becomes frightening?

Then ask: “What does this style help me do? What might it make harder?”

4

Use clear language

Naming death directly can make difficult conversations more understandable.

Why words matter

Euphemisms can feel gentler, but they can also create ambiguity. Clear words such as dying, died, and death can help people understand what is happening and make informed choices.

Ask instead of guessing

Do not assume what someone means by “a good death,” what they fear, what they believe, or what care they want. Ask open questions and listen for the answer.

Conversation starters

“If your health became much worse, what would matter most to you?”

Listen for priorities: comfort, alertness, being at home, being with particular people, independence, unfinished responsibilities, or something else.

“What are you most worried about?”

Do not rush to reassure. Fear can become more manageable once it is fully expressed and understood.

“If you couldn't speak for yourself, who would need to understand your wishes?”

This opens the door to advance care planning and sharing preferences with the people and professionals who may need to act on them.

“Would you rather focus on living as long as possible, or on comfort and quality of life if those goals conflict?”

There is no universal answer. These are personal values questions that deserve careful discussion with loved ones and healthcare professionals.

Plan before there is a crisis

Advance planning turns “I hope they know what I want” into clearer guidance.

A plan can answer practical questions

Where would you prefer to receive care if possible? Who should know your wishes? What matters most if treatments cannot restore the life you value? What should happen in an emergency? What would you want others to understand if you could not speak?

6

Step back and look again

When emotions are intense, our first interpretation may not be the whole picture.

From certainty → curiosity

Instead of “I know exactly what this means,” try “What else might be happening?” Curiosity creates room for new information.

From appearance → meaning

A behavior that looks confusing, passive, dramatic, or irrational may have a different meaning to the person experiencing it. Listen for the need underneath the behavior.

From fixing → accompanying

Sometimes the most helpful action is not solving the situation. It is being present, listening, naming what is happening, and helping someone feel safe.

From my priorities → their priorities

What looks unbearable to us may still hold meaning for the person living it. Let their priorities lead.

7

Legacy is more than possessions

We leave traces through relationships, stories, actions, values, and the ways we affect other people.

What you inherited

Think beyond money or objects. You may have inherited stories, habits, encouragement, values, skills, traditions, or ways of treating other people.

What you already gave

You may already have changed lives through ordinary acts: teaching, listening, caring, creating, raising someone, making a workplace kinder, or showing courage during hardship.

What you want to leave

A legacy can be intentional—letters, photographs, stories, a gift, a cause, a tradition—or simply the memory of how people felt when they were with you.

Start small

You do not need a grand project. One conversation, one letter, one photograph labeled with names, or one story recorded for someone you love can be meaningful.

8

Meaning, values, and connection

A meaningful life does not require one particular religion, philosophy, or worldview.

People make meaning through many frameworks: faith, relationships, nature, art, music, community, ethics, service, or a sense of connection to something larger than themselves. Near the end of life, people may review what their life has meant and what matters now.

The four messages

“I love you.”
“I’m sorry.”
“Please forgive me.”
“Thank you.”

Why they matter

These messages can express love, repair, regret, forgiveness, appreciation, and connection. You do not have to wait until someone is dying to say them.

Values check

Ask yourself: “If my life were a little more aligned with what I value, what would I do differently?”
